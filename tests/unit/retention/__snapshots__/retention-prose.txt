h2: The problem
p: Which of a shop's lapsed customers will order again, and would a win-back offer change that? The order history can answer the first question. Only a test with a holdout group, customers who get no offer, can answer the second.
p: Before either, the numbers have to be right. In this data, an order cancelled within 24 hours counted once as a sale and again as a return, and that made returned-order value look like it grew 29% in January to November 2011 against the same months of 2010, instead of about 2%.
h2: The data
p: The data is UCI Online Retail II, orders from a UK online gift retailer from 1 Dec 2009 to 9 Dec 2011: 1,067,371 invoice lines. Cleaning keeps cancellations apart as returns and drops lines without a customer, lines that are not products (such as postage) and lines with no positive quantity or price. After cleaning and the reversal rule below, 801,134 sales lines remain, in 36,452 orders from 5,850 customers.
h2: Method
p: I worked in five steps; most have their own period and their own group of customers:
li: Clean the invoice lines with SQL in DuckDB, and remove orders cancelled within 24 hours.
li: Group customers by the month of their first order and count, for each later month, the share who ordered in it.
li: Score every customer on recency, frequency and spend at a cutoff, and name segments from the scores.
li: Train scikit-learn models on an earlier cutoff and score them on a later one, against a simple rule.
li: Size a holdout test of a win-back offer for lapsed customers.

h2: Cohorts: who orders again, month by month
p: Customers are grouped by the month of their first order in the data. I use the January to November 2010 cohorts: December 2009 is the first month of the data, so it holds customers who were already buying, and December 2010 would end in the incomplete December 2011. A later month counts the cohort members who ordered in that month, so a share can rise again. Weighted by cohort size, about 20% order again in the month after their first order and about 18% in the twelfth month after it.

h2: Customer segments
p: At the September 2011 cutoff I score each customer on recency, frequency and spend by rank. Champions score 4 or 5 out of 5 on each of recency, frequency and spend (roughly the top two-fifths on each). They are 23% of customers and hold 71% of spend up to the cutoff, partly by construction.

h2: A model of who orders again
p: For the model, each customer's features use only orders up to a cutoff, and the label is whether they ordered again within the 90 days after it. Logistic regression and gradient boosting learn on an earlier cutoff, whose window closes before the later one, and are scored on the September 2011 cutoff against a rule that puts the most recent buyers first. In that window, 43.5% of customers ordered again.
h3: The model ranks repeat buyers only slightly better than recency
p: Gradient boosting ranks with a ROC AUC of 0.793, against 0.762 for putting the most recent buyers first: a gain of 0.03, with a 95% interval of 0.02 to 0.04 that covers test-sample variability only.

h2: Sizing a win-back test
p: Lapsed customers are those with no order in the 90 days before the September 2011 cutoff: 3,351 of them, and 29% of them ordered again within 90 days. I sized a possible holdout test; nobody was contacted and no test was run. With a standard two-group sample-size calculation (two-sided, a 5% significance level and 80% power), detecting a rise from 29% to 34%, an uplift of 5 percentage points, needs 1,353 lapsed customers in each group.
p: Two groups of that size are 2,706 customers, and 3,351 customers were lapsed at the cutoff, so one period has enough of them. That is arithmetic about how many customers there are, not evidence that an offer would work. A test on only the top 20% of lapsed customers by score would not fit: at their rate of ordering again, 53%, the same rise needs 1,547 customers per group, 3,094 in all, and that group has 670.
h3: Customers needed in each group of a holdout test
p: A sample-size calculation only: nobody was contacted and no test was run. Where the customers needed per group exceed the customers in that group, the test would need lapsed customers from more than one period.

h2: Findings
li: Removing orders cancelled within 24 hours changes the returns story: returned-order value grew about 2% in January to November 2011, not 29%. As a share of sales, returns went from 2.1% to 2.2%.
li: Across the January to November 2010 cohorts, about 20% order again in the month after their first order, and about 18% in the twelfth month after it. Repeat ordering varies by cohort, and a later month can be higher than an earlier one.
li: Champions are 23% of customers at the September 2011 cutoff and hold 71% of spend up to it, partly by construction.
li: Gradient boosting ranks who orders again only slightly better than putting the most recent buyers first: ROC AUC 0.793 against 0.762. Its scores rank customers but are not calibrated probabilities.
li: Sized to detect a rise of 5 percentage points, a holdout test of all lapsed customers needs 1,353 per group and fits in one period; a test on the top-scored customers alone does not.

p: The reversal rule as the cleaning SQL writes it, the same in both repositories at the linked commits:

A further test rebuilds the export from the downloaded dataset and expects the same bytes; it needs the dataset, so CI skips it.
li: Every figure on this page is marked with where it comes from: one of those exports, the source code at the linked commits, or a calculation. An audit of the built page fails on any unmarked number other than a calendar year.
li: The returns comparison uses the same months in both years, January to November, because December 2011 is incomplete.
li: The models are scored on a later cutoff than they learned from, so no order from the test window was used in training.
li: The gain in ROC AUC is 0.03, with a 95% interval of 0.02 to 0.04 from a paired bootstrap over the test customers, so it covers test-sample variability only.
li: This site's own sample-size code, which is tested against reference values from scipy and statsmodels, gives the same 1,353 per group as the analysis.
h2: Limits
li: This is historic, observational data from one UK gift retailer. It shows who ordered again, not how anyone would respond to a win-back offer. Nobody was contacted.
li: A higher score means a customer is more likely to order again, not more likely to be persuaded: rank is not uplift. Only a holdout test could show what an offer changes.
li: The interval on the AUC gain covers test-sample variability only. It does not cover retraining the model, another period or a change in the mix of customers.
li: The scores rank customers but are not calibrated probabilities: in the tenth of customers with the lowest scores, the gradient-boosting model predicted 3.1% on average and 8.7% ordered again.
li: Champions' share of spend is high partly by construction: spend is one of the three scores that define them.
li: The data starts in December 2009, so a first order in the data is not always a customer's first order, and lines without a customer number are left out of every customer figure.
